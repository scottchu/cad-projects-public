# Independent presentation-editor review — E-1

## Verdict

**A coherent, credible draft with a useful takeaway; editorial revision is recommended before delivery.** A first-time listener can understand “plan feedback from day one.” The deck is less successful at showing what working *side by side with an agent* actually looks like. Its examples make a stronger case for testing and observation than for the distinctive working relationship promised by the title.

The communication job is: help listeners plan an observation alongside their next action, and use the resulting evidence with an agent to choose the next step. Slides 5 and 13 do this best. The main improvement is to make one collaboration concrete and give the failures visible form, then shorten the repeated explanations around them.

Severity below means presentation impact, not a software or physical-acceptance finding.

## What the slides currently teach

Slides 1–3 state the subject, stakes and feedback loop clearly. Slide 4 finally supplies an object and task. Slide 5 pays this off: a 4.00 mm rod, several hole sizes, observed feel and a selected reference create a legible cause-and-consequence story. Slides 6–7 add independent checking and relationships between parts, but the audience must imagine both failures. Slide 8 expands to other tools; slides 9–10 then return to printing and distinguish file correctness, machine completion and useful physical results. Slides 11–12 introduce more process structures after the cases. Slide 13 converts the message into a practical example; slide 14 resolves the opening.

The argument is understandable without presenter notes. Its momentum weakens after slide 5 because too many subsequent slides explain lessons in the same prose-led shape. The audience receives several versions of the loop but little additional experience of people and agents using it.

## Top five priorities

### 1. Show one actual collaboration, including the changed decision — high

Slides 3, 8, 11 and 14 describe people and agents together. In the concrete cases, the agent's contribution largely disappears: slide 5 says the rod changed the design decision; slide 9 says inspection changed settings; slide 10 says assembly exposed failures. Who contributed what, and how did the observation return to the agent's work?

Make slide 5 the central worked example with a short, source-grounded sequence: **person's observation → agent's documented contribution → shared next decision**. Use existing evidence to name that contribution; do not manufacture a conversation or claim an agent performed an undocumented step. If the historical record cannot support that sequence, explicitly distinguish the observed case from a proposed way of working. Repeat this treatment only once in the print pilot. This directly earns the deck's title.

### 2. Let listeners see the important failures — high

Slide 4's exploded model shows components but does not reveal the desired wire shape or soldering task. Slide 6 says the wire path contradicted a 90° bend without showing the path. Slide 7 describes a shaft crossing solid gear material without showing the intersection. Slide 10 names “fit and mechanism-design failures” without identifying a concrete failure.

Add a simple task sketch on slide 4 and an annotated, evidence-backed image or schematic for the strongest failure case. For slide 6, compare the required bend with the actual recorded erroneous path. For slide 10, show one documented assembly observation and what remained unresolved. These are teaching visuals, not decorative CAD renders. If usable evidence is unavailable, shorten the unsupported visual burden rather than inventing geometry or a physical outcome. Slide 7 is the first candidate to cut from the main sequence if it cannot add a distinct, visible lesson.

### 3. Reduce the number of process explanations — medium

Slides 3, 11, 12 and 13 all describe organizing actions, evidence and decisions. Their distinctions are real, but first-time listeners are asked to learn a feedback loop, printer responsibility split, review workflow and planning table in one short talk. Slide 12's “Candidate,” “Formal review” and separate critique/validation boxes especially shift attention toward internal procedure.

Keep slide 3 as the single conceptual model and slide 13 as its worked application. Fold slide 11's job continuity into the print case. Attach slide 12's independence lesson to slide 6 in plain language: a fresh check must return to the original requirement. Keep the full role diagram as supporting material if needed. Introduce the wire case sooner so the opening has an object before its third abstract explanation.

### 4. Make the broader-tools section extend a completed case — medium

Slide 8 interrupts the sequence between CAD cases and the real print pilot. Its camera/sensor/robotic-arm descriptions are clear but read as a catalog. “Manipulate a part, then observe the effect” leaves the observation mechanism unspecified.

Move this slide after the print outcome, where human touch and machine status have already demonstrated two different observations. Use one clearly labeled illustrative loop spanning action, measurement and next decision, with cameras/sensors/arms as possible tools within it. Retain the visible distinction between demonstrated work and broader possibilities. This gives the digital/physical scope a reason to expand without implying completed integrations.

### 5. Give the audience one portable sentence to keep — medium

Slide 13 is the most useful takeaway, but “compare the observation with the intended fit” is less concrete than slide 5's actual scale of tightness, sliding and wobble. Slide 14 has a memorable three-line instruction, while its title returns to a general statement about learning.

Make slide 13 a completed example of a reusable sentence: **“Before we act, agree what we expect, what we will observe, and what would make us change the plan.”** Use the existing rod evidence to make the decision criterion tangible. Close with this instruction or the existing “Design the action / Design how to observe its effect / Use the feedback to decide together.” No audience exercise or question is necessary.

## Keep

- The clear title and consistent day-one emphasis; the closing resolves the opening.
- Slide 5's measured choices, highlighted reference and bounded claim. This is the anchor of the talk.
- The separation of digital checks, printer completion and physical assembly evidence, especially slide 10.
- The honest labels identifying historical models, proposed work and undemonstrated integrations.
- The restrained palette, strong type hierarchy and readable body copy. The issue is repetitive composition, not a need for a wholesale visual redesign.
