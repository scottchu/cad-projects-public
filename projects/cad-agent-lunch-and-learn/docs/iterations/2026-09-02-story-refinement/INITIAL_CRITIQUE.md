## Audience reading

The talk argues that people and agents should plan feedback before acting across digital and physical tools. The rod test establishes why physical evidence matters; the shaft repair gives the clearest collaboration example; incorrect and incomplete digital checks show why apparent agreement is insufficient; the printing pilot separates preparation, machine completion, and usable results. Future sensors could extend this same loop. That argument is understandable and factual boundaries are unusually clear.

The remaining weakness is cumulative storytelling. The middle can feel like successive examples of “checks missed something,” while the diagrams sometimes label the cases without showing their decisive relationships. The opening and ending are strong enough to retain. No audience questions or controls are needed.

## Actionable findings

1. **P1 — Make the actual collaboration unambiguous (slide 5).** “Agents’ documented work” is followed by imperative verbs: “Identify… Enlarge… regenerate and check.” A reader cannot tell whether this happened or is an instruction. Use documented past tense, explicitly retaining the shaft: agents identified the relevant parts, enlarged only the two plate holes, regenerated the files, and passed digital checks. Label the third column “Pending physical check,” with reprinting/reuse/testing as future actions. This is the only concrete, attributed agent contribution; its completion status should not depend on the footer.

2. **P1 — Show failure and bounded repair in the shaft diagram (slide 5).** The shaft silhouette, right-pointing arrow, and matching hole silhouettes currently suggest successful insertion. Show the failed passage with a stop marker or “Before: shaft will not pass,” then identify the two holes as the only repair targets. Label the shaft unchanged. Do not depict a successful physical insertion: the retest is pending. This makes the picture explain why the observation led to that specific agent task.

3. **P2 — Give the jig image enough anatomy to earn its space (slide 3).** The wire sketch explains the intended result well. The exploded model does not identify which members are forming rods, which are guide plates, or where the later center shaft belongs. Add two or three source-verified callouts to the existing image and one short functional connection: rods shape the bend, plates keep their positions. Introduce the center shaft at slide 5 if labeling it here would overburden the introduction. Readers should not have to infer the relationship between the left sketch and the right object.

4. **P2 — Make the nominal fit visible (slide 4).** The table convincingly explains the empirical decision, but the cutaway does not clearly expose the gap advertised as enlarged ×8. Add a leader pointing to the actual visible gap, or adjust the cutaway/view so that separation is obvious. Say “hole diameter exceeds rod diameter by 0.05 mm” if needed to avoid the ambiguous word “clearance.” Clearly distinguish the nominal 4.05 mm geometry pictured from the tested 4.15 mm reference highlighted in the table. Preserve the qualitative physical results and the limited applicability of the sample.

5. **P1 — Turn the middle into an argument that accumulates (slides 6–10).** Slide 6 establishes a loop; slide 7 restarts with another problem. Add a short bridge such as “The loop helps only if its evidence tests the right thing.” Then give each case a distinct contribution: slide 7 checks must return independently to intent; slide 8 checks must include relationships between parts; slide 9 tool output must match the requested job; slide 10 completion must be connected to real use. These can replace existing generic lines rather than add text. Introduce slide 9 as carrying the same requirement beyond CAD into print preparation. Preserve the explicit separation of its physical failure from the center-shaft case.

6. **P2 — Explain the shared-assumption mechanism visually (slide 7).** The curve-versus-diagonal comparison shows the mismatch, but not how two checks agreed. Make the single wrong reference visibly feed both the model and its test, with the required 90° bend outside that shared path. A small branching relationship is enough; no new engineering schematic is needed. Promote “wire-only correction proposed; not completed” into readable status adjacent to the next action instead of relying on small footer copy.

7. **P2 — Make Kage’s consequence and proposed response connect (slide 8).** The overlap drawing is already explanatory and should stay. Put “Found in a digital check” near the finding so it cannot be mistaken for a physical assembly failure. “One moving ring and one drive” introduces components that are never shown and does not directly explain prevention of omitted part-pair checks. Prefer a plain-language proposed restart that tests each small mechanism and its connections before combining the clock. Do not imply that restart tests ran.

8. **P2 — Simplify the print-preparation story (slide 9).** The correction appears twice: “Complete profiles restored…” and “Complete profiles produced the corrected file…”. Retain one causal sequence: requested head 3 → first print file selected head 1 because settings were incomplete → file inspection caught it → complete settings produced the corrected file before printing. Explain “profiles” as printer/material settings. PETG, G-code, T2/T0, and U1 add vocabulary without helping the visible lesson; remove unnecessary codes or expand only what is retained. The head comparison is sufficient evidence for the audience.

9. **P2 — Separate the two next-step obligations (slide 10).** “Not accepted” is clear, but the next action jumps from fit/mechanism failure to checking preparation with “test fixtures.” That can imply that fixing preparation fixes the physical design. State that pipeline repeatability remained a separate next step, using small known test pieces, while the pilot’s physical result remained unaccepted and its exact defect was unspecified. Do not invent a design diagnosis or repair. This distinction should be main copy, not only a disclaimer.

10. **P2 — Give the future loop an observable event (slide 11).** The arm has no pictured insertion target; its tip bears an unexplained X. Camera and force icons then ask generic questions. Show an illustrative rod and guide at the arm, label the intended small insertion, and identify an illustrative observation such as “movement stops while force rises.” Connect that observation to a conditional response, without a numerical threshold or claimed successful integration. Label the joint interpretation as people + agents. Keep future/proposed status conspicuous. This would demonstrate what additional tools contribute rather than simply naming them.

11. **P2 — Keep planning distinct from hindsight (slide 12).** “Before we act” frames an advance agreement, but “Keep as the reference: 4.15 mm” is already a historical outcome. Separate the first three rows as a worked planning template and the last row as “What the recorded test taught us.” Clarify “check… again in the real guide” as confirming the sample-derived choice in the intended assembly. This is a useful callback, provided it teaches a reusable habit rather than replaying slide 4’s result.

12. **P3 — Final copy polish.** Expand or replace the first “CAD” (slide 1) for a broad audience. In slide 12 the subtitle repeats the title closely; let it introduce the worked example instead. Preserve the closing three actions and day-one line on slide 13: their repetition functions as a memorable conclusion, not a new problem.
